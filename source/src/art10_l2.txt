hook: Draw a friend's face and the eyes often end up near the top of the head. In reality, eyes sit about halfway down. Artists don't guess proportions; they measure them by sight with a pencil held at arm's length.
sc: I can use sighting to compare sizes and angles | I can find proportions using a unit of measure | I can correct a drawing by checking proportions
v: proportion :: The size of one part compared to another part or the whole.
v: sighting :: Measuring by holding a pencil at arm's length to compare sizes and angles.
v: unit of measure :: One part of the subject used to measure all the others, such as the height of a head.
v: foreshortening :: When an object pointing toward the viewer looks shorter than it really is.
v: plumb line :: An imaginary vertical line used to check alignment.
v: negative space :: The space around and between objects.
ts: Measure with Your Eye || Studio task || How do artists measure proportion by sight?
tx: Sighting method: Stand in the same spot, arm fully straight, one eye closed. Hold the pencil vertically or horizontally. Line the tip up with one edge of an object and slide your thumb to the other edge. That length is your unit. ¶ Part 1, Practice (10 minutes): Sight a classroom door. How many door widths fit in its height? (Often about two and a half.) Check with a tape measure afterwards. ¶ Part 2, Still life (25 minutes): Set up a bottle, a mug and a box. Choose the mug's height as the unit. Sight: How many mugs tall is the bottle? How wide is the box in mug units? Mark these on your paper lightly before drawing. Use the pencil to check angles of tilted edges, and use plumb lines to see what lines up vertically. ¶ Part 3, Figure proportions (10 minutes): An adult standing figure is about seven and a half heads tall. The eyes are halfway down the head. The elbows line up with the waist; the fingertips reach mid-thigh. Sketch a classmate posing for two minutes using head units. ¶ Part 4, Check (5 minutes): Turn your drawing upside down or look in a mirror. Errors jump out. Correct one.

=== ART10.A1 2
hook: A circle on paper is flat. Add a shadow, a highlight and a little reflected light, and suddenly it's a ball you could pick up. That's the power of value: the range from light to dark that makes forms look three-dimensional.
sc: I can identify the parts of light on a form | I can render a form using a full value range | I can use hatching, cross-hatching and blending
v: value :: How light or dark a tone is.
v: highlight :: The brightest spot where light hits directly.
v: core shadow :: The darkest part of the shadow on the form itself.
v: cast shadow :: The shadow a form throws onto a nearby surface.
v: reflected light :: Light bounced back onto the shadow side from nearby surfaces.
v: rendering :: Shading a drawing to show form, light and texture.
ts: Light on Form || Studio task || How does value turn flat shapes into 3-D forms?
tx: Materials: 2H, HB, 2B and 6B pencils; blending stump; kneaded eraser; white egg, ball or cylinder; one desk lamp. ¶ Part 1, Parts of light (10 minutes): Light one white ball from one side. Find and label: highlight, light side, half-tone (mid value), core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow. Note: the cast shadow is darkest right where the ball touches the table. ¶ Part 2, Value scale (10 minutes): Make a 7-step scale from white to the darkest dark. Make one with hatching (parallel lines), one with cross-hatching and one blended. ¶ Part 3, Render (25 minutes): Draw the egg or ball lit by the lamp. Lightly map the shapes of light and shadow first, then build values gradually from light to dark. Keep the highlight as clean paper. Lift highlights with a kneaded eraser if needed. Shade reflected light lighter than the core shadow but darker than the light side. ¶ Part 4, Critique (5 minutes): Squint at your drawing beside the real object. Squinting simplifies values. Is your darkest dark dark enough?

hook: Walk into a fast-food restaurant: red and yellow everywhere. Walk into a spa: soft blues and greens. Designers choose colour schemes on purpose because colour changes how we feel before we even notice it.
sc: I can identify monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split-complementary and triadic schemes | I can explain how warm and cool colours affect mood | I can plan an artwork with a chosen scheme
v: colour scheme :: A planned group of colours used together.
v: monochromatic :: One hue with its tints, tones and shades.
v: analogous :: Colours next to each other on the wheel.
v: complementary :: Colours opposite each other on the wheel.
v: triadic :: Three colours evenly spaced on the wheel.
v: warm and cool colours :: Reds, oranges and yellows feel warm; blues, greens and violets feel cool.

tx: Part 1, Scheme gallery (10 minutes): Look at examples: Picasso's Blue Period (monochromatic blue, sadness), Van Gogh's "Café Terrace at Night" (complementary yellow and blue), a sunset photo (analogous warm). Name each scheme and the mood. ¶ Part 2, Five schemes (20 minutes): Draw one simple line drawing (for example a window with a plant) five times as small thumbnails. Paint or colour each with a different scheme: monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split-complementary (one colour plus the two beside its complement, such as blue with yellow-orange and red-orange), triadic (such as red, yellow, blue). ¶ Part 3, Warm vs cool (10 minutes): Paint the same thumbnail once in warm colours and once in cool. Which feels like morning? Which feels like night? ¶ Part 4, Plan (10 minutes): Choose a mood (calm, tense, joyful, lonely). Choose the scheme that best fits and justify it in two sentences for your final composition next lesson.

=== ART10.B1 2
hook: Two paintings can use the same colours and still look completely different, because one is organized and the other isn't. The principles of design (balance, emphasis, movement, contrast, unity) are the tools artists use to organize what's on the page.
sc: I can name and identify the principles of design | I can plan a composition with a clear focal point and balance | I can paint a composition that combines colour and design principles
v: principles of design :: Ways of organizing elements: balance, contrast, emphasis, movement, pattern, rhythm, unity, proportion.
v: focal point :: The area that attracts the eye first.
v: balance :: How visual weight is distributed (symmetrical, asymmetrical, radial).
v: rule of thirds :: Dividing the picture into a 3 × 3 grid and placing key elements on the lines or intersections.
v: movement :: The path the viewer's eye takes through a work.
v: unity :: The sense that all parts belong together.
ts: Putting It Together || Studio task || How do design principles organize a painting?
tx: Part 1, Principles hunt (10 minutes): Look at three paintings (for example Hokusai's "The Great Wave," Mary Pratt's still lifes of jelly jars, and a Lawren Harris landscape). For each, find the focal point, the type of balance and how the eye moves. ¶ Part 2, Thumbnails (15 minutes): Using the colour scheme from last lesson, draw four thumbnails of your subject: centred, rule of thirds, diagonal movement, and high-contrast focal point. Choose the strongest. ¶ Part 3, Paint (35 minutes or across classes): Transfer the composition to your final surface. Block in large shapes first, then build up values and details. Keep the strongest contrast and most detail at the focal point. Use repeated colours across the painting for unity. ¶ Part 4, Check (5 minutes): Look from 3 m away. Where does your eye go first? Does it travel through the painting? Make one adjustment.
