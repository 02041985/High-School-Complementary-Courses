=== ART10.C1 2
hook: Art critics, gallery labels and university essays all do the same thing: turn looking into clear writing. A critical response isn't a list of likes and dislikes. It's an argument about an artwork, supported with evidence you can point to.
sc: I can plan a critical response with a clear claim | I can support my interpretation with visual evidence and context | I can write in a formal, respectful voice
v: critical response :: Writing that analyzes and interprets an artwork and makes a supported judgment.
v: thesis :: The main claim of a piece of writing.
v: visual evidence :: Specific details in an artwork that support a point.
v: formal analysis :: Discussion of the elements and principles of design.
v: interpretation :: An explanation of meaning.
v: art vocabulary :: Specific terms such as contrast, composition and texture.
ts: Writing About Art || Writing workshop || How do I write a clear, supported critical response?
tx: Structure (five paragraphs, 400–500 words): ¶ 1. Introduction: title, artist, date, medium, one sentence of context, and a thesis. Example: "In 'The Great Wave off Kanagawa,' Hokusai uses contrast in scale and dynamic line to show the power of nature over human life." ¶ 2. Description: what is shown, without opinion. ¶ 3. Formal analysis: two or three elements or principles with specific visual evidence ("the claw-like foam at the crest," "the tiny Mount Fuji framed by the wave"). ¶ 4. Interpretation: meaning, supported by evidence and context (Japan's relationship with nature, the popularity of prints). ¶ 5. Judgment and conclusion: is the work successful? By what criteria? Restate the thesis in new words. ¶ Style: present tense, formal voice (no "I think it's cool"), art vocabulary, quotes of short labels or titles only. ¶ Activity: Read a weak paragraph ("This painting is really nice and the colours are good") and a strong one; list what makes the strong one better. Then plan your own response with the organizer and draft the thesis and analysis paragraph.

v: distortion :: Changing shapes or proportions on purpose for effect.
v: mark-making :: The different lines, dots and textures an artist makes.
v: symbolic colour :: Colour chosen for meaning rather than realism.
v: gesture :: Energetic, expressive marks showing movement or emotion.
ts: Make Feelings Visible || Studio task || How do artists express emotion through visual choices?
tx: Part 1, Look (10 minutes): Compare Munch's "The Scream" (1893), Van Gogh's "The Starry Night" (1889) and an Emily Carr forest painting. hook: Next to every artwork in a gallery is a small block of text. In 100 words or so, the artist statement tells viewers what the work is about and why it was made, without explaining away the mystery.
sc: I can explain the purpose of an artist statement | I can write a clear, concise statement about my artwork | I can revise my statement using feedback
v: artist statement :: A short text explaining an artwork's idea, process and intent.
v: intent :: What the artist hoped to communicate.
v: process :: How the artwork was made.
v: concise :: Clear and brief, with no unnecessary words.
v: first person :: Writing with "I" and "my."
v: reflection :: Thinking about what you learned and what worked.
ts: In My Own Words || Writing workshop || How do artists explain their work in a clear statement?
tx: An artist statement answers: What is the work about? (concept) How did I make it? (materials and process) Why these choices? (intent) Sometimes: what do I hope viewers feel or think? ¶ Model statement (original): "Weight. Acrylic and collage on board, 2026. My backpack has carried me through ten years of school. In this painting, I stacked collage papers from old tests and timetables until the backpack seems ready to burst, and painted the background in a cramped, cool palette. I distorted the straps to stretch across the whole surface, so the viewer feels the pull. I want the viewer to think about what young people carry, and what we might put down." (90 words) ¶ Tips: first person, present tense, specific details, 75–150 words, avoid "I just liked it" and long technical lists, don't tell viewers exactly what to feel. ¶ Activity 1 (10 minutes): Read two statements, one vague and one specific. List differences. ¶ Activity 2 (20 minutes): Draft your statement for your expressive artwork. ¶ Activity 3 (15 minutes): Peer feedback: highlight one sentence that helps you understand the work and one that's vague. Revise. ¶ Activity 4 (5 minutes): Read your final statement aloud next to your artwork.
