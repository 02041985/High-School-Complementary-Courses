hook: Painters can make a red apple glow, a sky feel cold or a shadow feel full of colour, all with only a few tubes of paint. The secret isn't buying more colours. It's knowing how to mix them and how to control light and dark.
sc: I can mix secondary and tertiary colours from primaries | I can build a value scale with tints and shades | I can use complementary or analogous colours on purpose in a composition
v: hue :: The name of a colour, such as red, blue or yellow-green.
v: tint :: A hue mixed with white to make it lighter.
v: shade :: A hue mixed with black or its complement to make it darker.
v: complementary colours :: Colours opposite each other on the colour wheel, such as red and green.
v: analogous colours :: Colours next to each other on the colour wheel, such as blue, blue-green and green.
v: value :: How light or dark a colour is.
v: composition :: How the parts of an artwork are arranged.
ts: Colour Wheel and Value Studio || Studio task || How do painters control colour and value?
tx: Materials: acrylic or tempera paint in red, yellow, blue, white and black; brushes; palette; water; heavy paper. ¶ Part 1, Colour wheel (20 minutes): Paint a 12-part wheel. Start with the primaries (red, yellow, blue), mix the secondaries (orange, green, violet), then the tertiaries (red-orange, yellow-green and so on). Add small amounts of the darker colour to the lighter one. ¶ Part 2, Value scale (15 minutes): Choose one hue. Paint a nine-step scale: four tints (add white), the pure hue, and four shades (add black on one side; on a second strip, darken with the complement instead and compare). ¶ Part 3, Colour schemes (10 minutes): Paint two small thumbnails of the same simple scene (for example a mug on a table): one with a complementary scheme, one with an analogous scheme. ¶ Part 4, Reflect (5 minutes): Which scheme feels calmer? Which feels more energetic? Why? ¶ Tip: Darkening with the complement gives richer shadows than black, which can make colours look muddy.

hook: Two people can look at the same painting and see completely different things. A critique isn't about saying "I like it" or "I don't." It's a step-by-step way of looking that lets you back up what you think with what you see.
sc: I can describe an artwork objectively | I can analyze how the elements and principles are used | I can interpret meaning and judge an artwork with evidence
v: critique :: A structured discussion that describes, analyzes, interprets and judges an artwork.
v: describe :: To list what you see without giving opinions.
v: analyze :: To explain how the elements and principles of design are organized.
v: interpretation :: An explanation of what an artwork means, backed by evidence.
v: symbolism :: Using an object, colour or image to stand for an idea.
v: context :: The time, place and culture in which an artwork was made.
v: aesthetic :: Ideas about beauty and what makes art meaningful or successful.
ts: Four Steps to Seeing || Critique || How can we look closely at art from any culture and respond with evidence?
tx: Choose three artworks from different cultures and times, for example: a Haida bentwood box or button blanket, Katsushika Hokusai's "The Great Wave off Kanagawa" (Japan, about 1831), and a work by a contemporary Canadian artist. ¶ Step 1, Describe (What do I see?): List subject, colours, lines, shapes, textures, medium and size. No opinions yet. ¶ Step 2, Analyze (How is it organized?): Which elements and principles stand out? Where is the focal point? How does the eye move? How is balance or rhythm created? ¶ Step 3, Interpret (What does it mean?): What mood or message does it suggest? What symbols appear? What does the context (culture, time, purpose) add? Use "I think ... because I see ..." ¶ Step 4, Judge (Is it successful?): Decide whether the work succeeds and by which criteria: skill, expression, originality or function. ¶ Respect: When looking at Indigenous and other cultural works, learn what the work means to the culture that made it, use the correct names, and remember that some objects are ceremonial, not decorative.

hook: Artists rarely start with a perfect idea. Picasso made dozens of sketches for "Guernica." The sketchbook is where messy ideas get tested, changed and turned into something worth making.
sc: I can generate many ideas from a prompt | I can develop one idea through thumbnails and studies | I can reflect on choices in my sketchbook
v: sketchbook :: A book for recording ideas, studies and experiments.
v: ideation :: The process of generating many ideas.
v: thumbnail :: A small, quick sketch used to test a composition.
v: concept :: The main idea behind an artwork.
v: mind map :: A diagram that connects a central idea to related ideas.
v: reflection :: Thinking and writing about your choices and what you learned.
ts: From Prompt to Plan || Studio task || How do artists turn a simple prompt into a strong idea?
tx: Prompt: "Something I carry" (an object, a memory, a responsibility or a feeling). ¶ Part 1, Mind map (10 minutes): Write the prompt in the centre of a sketchbook page. Branch out with at least 15 words, objects, places, people and feelings. Circle three that surprise you. ¶ Part 2, Visual research (10 minutes): Collect references: draw two objects from life, take or find two photos, and note one artist who works with a similar idea. ¶ Part 3, Thumbnails (15 minutes): Draw six thumbnails (5 cm by 7 cm) showing different compositions: close-up, bird's-eye view, unusual crop, symbolic object, combined images, empty space. ¶ Part 4, Develop (10 minutes): Choose the strongest thumbnail. Make a larger study testing value or colour. ¶ Part 5, Reflect (5 minutes): Annotate the page: Why this idea? What will you change? What feeling do you want the viewer to have?
