hook: Syncopation is what makes music groove: the accent lands where you don't expect it, between the beats. Funk, Latin, jazz and almost every pop song depend on it, and band music is full of it too.
sc: I can count syncopated rhythms with subdivision | I can perform rhythms with ties and off-beat accents accurately | I can use a sight-reading routine before playing
v: syncopation :: Stressing a weak beat or the off-beat instead of the strong beat.
v: subdivision :: Dividing the beat into smaller parts, such as "1 & 2 &."
v: tie :: A curved line joining two notes of the same pitch so they are held as one.
v: off-beat :: The "&" between beats.
v: sight-reading :: Playing music you have not seen before.
v: road map :: The order of repeats, endings and jumps in a piece.
ts: Between the Beats || Rhythm workshop || How do musicians read and feel syncopated rhythms?
tx: Part 1, Subdivide (10 minutes): With a metronome at 80, count "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &" aloud while tapping the beat with your foot. Clap only the "&s." ¶ Part 2, Pattern ladder (15 minutes): Clap and count each pattern in 4/4, then play it on one note: ¶ (a) quarter, quarter, quarter, quarter (no syncopation) ¶ (b) eighth, quarter, eighth, quarter, quarter (the "1 &(2)" figure: accent on "&" of 1, held through 2) ¶ (c) eighth, quarter, quarter, quarter, eighth (accents fall on "&s") ¶ (d) quarter tied to an eighth across beat 3 (the tie hides beat 3) ¶ Rule: Write the counts under each note before you play. Ties: count the second note but don't play it. ¶ Part 3, Sight-reading routine "STARS" (10 minutes): S: sharps and flats (key signature), T: time signature and tempo, A: accidentals, R: rhythms (circle the tricky ones and count them), S: signs (dynamics, articulations, repeats, road map). Spend 60 seconds on STARS, then play without stopping. ¶ Part 4, Band read (15 minutes): Sight-read a short exercise as a band using STARS. Keep going even if you make a mistake.

hook: A band of 40 players can sound like one giant instrument or like 40 people playing at the same time. The difference is balance and blend: listening more than you play.
sc: I can balance my sound within the ensemble pyramid | I can blend with my section by matching tone, volume and pitch | I can play straight and swing eighths and hear the difference
v: balance :: The right volume relationship between parts so all are heard as intended.
v: blend :: When individual sounds merge so no single player sticks out.
v: ensemble pyramid :: A model where low instruments play with the most sound and high instruments with less.
v: intonation :: How in tune a player is with others.
v: straight eighths :: Eighth notes played evenly.
v: swing :: A feel where eighth notes are played long-short, like a triplet.
ts: Building the Pyramid || Ensemble rehearsal || How does a band balance and blend into one sound?
tx: The ensemble pyramid: Imagine a pyramid. The base is wide: tubas, baritones, bass clarinets, bari saxes. The middle: horns, trombones, tenor saxes, clarinets. The top is narrow: flutes, trumpets, alto saxes. Low instruments carry more sound; high instruments sit on top. If the top is louder than the base, the band sounds thin and shrill. ¶ Part 1, Build a chord (15 minutes): Play a B-flat concert chord. Basses start, then each section enters one at a time from the bottom up. Each section should be able to hear the section below it. ¶ Part 2, Section blend (10 minutes): Each section plays a long tone. Rule: if you can hear yourself above your neighbour, play softer. Match the tone and start and end together. ¶ Part 3, Intonation (10 minutes): Use a drone. Listen for "beats" (wobbles); adjust until they disappear. ¶ Part 4, Straight vs swing (15 minutes): Play a B-flat scale in eighths, straight. Then swing it: long-short, like "doo-ba doo-ba," with the long note two thirds and the short note one third of the beat. Accent the off-beats lightly. Play a short jazz chart excerpt with swing feel.

hook: Play a melody with every note at the same volume and it sounds like a ringtone. Shape it, growing toward the high point and relaxing at the end, and it sounds like someone speaking. That shaping is called phrasing.
sc: I can identify phrases and their high points | I can shape phrases with dynamics and breathing | I can follow concert etiquette and show stage presence
v: phrase :: A musical sentence, often four or eight bars long.
v: dynamics :: Levels of loud and soft, such as p, mf and f.
v: crescendo :: Gradually getting louder.
v: breath mark :: A comma-like sign showing where to breathe.
v: stage presence :: How a performer looks and behaves on stage.
v: concert etiquette :: Expected behaviour for performers and audiences at a concert.
ts: Musical Sentences || Performance task || How do musicians shape phrases and present a concert?
tx: Part 1, Speak it (5 minutes): Read this sentence flat, then with natural expression: "I can't believe we actually won the championship." Where did your voice rise? Music works the same way. ¶ Part 2, Find the phrase (10 minutes): In your concert piece, mark phrases with a bracket over each four- or eight-bar section. Circle the high point (often the highest or longest note, or where harmony is most tense). ¶ Part 3, Shape it (15 minutes): Plan the dynamics: grow toward the high point and relax after it, even if the music only says "mf." Mark breath marks at phrase ends, never in the middle of a phrase. Play with a three-level plan: start, peak, end. ¶ Part 4, Concert etiquette (10 minutes): Performers: walk on and off confidently; instruments up together on the conductor's signal; no talking or fidgeting; stay still at the end until the conductor lowers the arms; bow together. Audience: enter and leave between pieces; applaud at the end of a piece, not between movements; phones off. ¶ Part 5, Run (10 minutes): Perform one piece as if it were the concert, from walk-on to bow.
