hook: The golden rule of improv comes from comedy theatres like The Second City in Toronto and Chicago: "Yes, and." Say no to your partner's idea and the scene dies. Say "yes, and" and the scene can go anywhere.
sc: I can accept my partner's offers | I can build on offers by adding new information | I can commit to a character with a clear objective
v: improvisation :: Acting without a script, creating the scene in the moment.
v: offer :: Anything a performer says or does that gives the scene information.
v: accept :: To treat a partner's offer as true in the scene.
v: block :: To reject or ignore an offer, which stops the scene.
v: objective :: What a character wants in a scene.
v: commitment :: Fully believing in and playing your choices.
ts: Yes, And || Scene work || How do improvisers build a scene together without a script?
tx: Warm-up, Word at a time story (5 minutes): In a circle, tell a story one word per person. Keep it moving. ¶ Game 1, "No, but" vs "Yes, and" (10 minutes): In pairs, Partner A suggests a plan for a trip. Round 1: B answers every line with "No, but..." Round 2: B answers every line with "Yes, and..." Compare how each round felt. ¶ Game 2, Gift giving (10 minutes): A mimes handing B a box. B opens it and names what's inside ("A live penguin!"). A accepts and adds ("I knew you'd love him, he needs feeding every hour"). ¶ Game 3, Objective scenes (20 minutes): Pairs draw a location card (bus stop, hospital waiting room, campsite) and each secretly draws an objective (get the other person to leave, borrow money, confess a secret). Improvise for two minutes using "Yes, and," pursuing your objective without blocking. ¶ Debrief: Which offers moved the scene forward? Where did someone block?

hook: Imagine a scene where the most important moment happens with the actor's back to the audience. Nobody sees it. Blocking, the planned movement of actors on stage, makes sure the audience sees what matters.
sc: I can name the nine stage areas | I can follow and write blocking using stage directions | I can move on stage so the audience can see and hear me
v: blocking :: The planned movement and positions of actors on stage.
v: upstage :: The part of the stage farthest from the audience.
v: downstage :: The part of the stage closest to the audience.
v: stage left :: The actor's left when facing the audience.
v: cheat out :: To turn the body partly toward the audience while still relating to other actors.
v: sightline :: The audience's line of view to the stage.
v: proscenium :: A stage framed by an arch, with the audience on one side.
ts: Map the Stage || Scene work || How do actors use stage areas and blocking so the audience can follow the story?
tx: Stage map (proscenium stage): The stage is divided into nine areas, named from the actor's point of view facing the audience: ¶ Upstage Right (UR) | Upstage Centre (UC) | Upstage Left (UL) ¶ Centre Right (CR) | Centre (C) | Centre Left (CL) ¶ Downstage Right (DR) | Downstage Centre (DC) | Downstage Left (DL) ¶ Why "upstage"? Old stages were raked (sloped up away from the audience), so walking away meant walking up. ¶ Activity 1, Stage director says (10 minutes): The teacher calls areas; everyone moves there. Speed up. ¶ Activity 2, Blocking notation (10 minutes): Learn shorthand: X = cross, DSL = downstage left, Ent. = enter, Ex. = exit. Example: "X DL to window" means cross to downstage left to the window. ¶ Activity 3, Block a scene (25 minutes): In pairs, take a short two-person scene. Write blocking in the margin for each line. Rules: strongest moments downstage or centre; cheat out; don't stand in a straight line with your partner; motivate every move. Run it for another pair who checks sightlines.

=== DR10.D1 0
hook: For every actor you see on stage, there may be ten people you never see: designers, builders, operators and the stage manager calling every cue. A show can survive a forgotten line. It can't survive a missed blackout or a set that falls over.
sc: I can describe the main production roles | I can explain what cues are and how they are called | I can operate basic lighting or sound safely
v: stage manager :: The person who runs rehearsals and calls all cues during performances.
v: cue :: A signal for a lighting, sound or scene change.
v: lighting designer :: The person who designs how the stage is lit.
v: sound designer :: The person who plans music and sound effects.
v: crew :: People who move sets and props and run the show backstage.
v: technical rehearsal :: A rehearsal focused on running lights, sound and scene changes.
v: prompt book :: The stage manager's script with all blocking and cues.
ts: Behind the Scenes || Production task || What do technical theatre roles do, and how do they work together?
tx: Production roles: Director (overall vision), stage manager (runs rehearsals, keeps the prompt book, calls cues), lighting designer and operator, sound designer and operator, set designer and builders, costume designer, props master, house manager (audience and front of house). ¶ Part 1, Role jigsaw (10 minutes): Each group researches one role and teaches the class: main job, key skills, when they work (rehearsal or performance). ¶ Part 2, Cue sheet (15 minutes): Read a short scene and write a cue sheet. Each cue has a number, a trigger (a line or action), and what happens. Example: LX 1 (lighting), trigger "Lights up," state: warm general wash. SQ 1 (sound), trigger "She opens the window," effect: birdsong. ¶ Part 3, Calling cues (15 minutes): The stage manager says "Stand by LX 2," the operator answers "Standing by," then "LX 2, go." Practise with the lighting board or a simple app and a speaker. ¶ Safety: never look directly into lights, never touch hot lamps, keep cables taped down, wear closed-toe shoes backstage.
