hook: In the 1890s, Russian director Konstantin Stanislavski grew tired of actors who declaimed lines and struck poses. He asked a simple question: what if actors behaved as real people would in those exact circumstances? That question still shapes almost every film and TV performance you watch.
sc: I can find a character's given circumstances in a script | I can use the "magic if" to make choices | I can identify objectives and beats in a scene
v: Stanislavski's system :: An approach to acting that seeks truthful behaviour in imaginary circumstances.
v: given circumstances :: The facts of a scene: who, where, when, what happened before and what is happening now.
v: magic if :: Asking "What would I do if I were in this situation?"
v: objective :: What a character wants in a scene.
v: beat :: A section of a scene where one objective or tactic is played; a new beat starts when it changes.
v: subtext :: The thoughts and feelings beneath the words.
ts: The Detective's Script || Script analysis || How do actors use the script to find truthful choices?
tx: Scene (original, for this lesson): ¶ A kitchen, 11 p.m. MAYA (17) is at the table with a university acceptance letter. Her FATHER enters in his work uniform. ¶ FATHER: You're still up. ¶ MAYA: Couldn't sleep. ¶ FATHER: Your mother said there was mail. ¶ MAYA: Just flyers. (She slides the letter under a placemat.) ¶ FATHER: The shop called again. They want me on Saturdays too. ¶ MAYA: That's good, right? More hours. ¶ FATHER: More hours. (Pause.) You should sleep. ¶ MAYA: Dad? (He turns.) Nothing. Goodnight. ¶ Part 1, Given circumstances (15 minutes): Answer the five W's for each character using only evidence in the script, then add inferences (marked "I think"). ¶ Part 2, Objectives and beats (15 minutes): Mark where each beat changes. Write an objective as an active verb for each beat ("to protect him," "to get her to talk"). ¶ Part 3, Magic if (5 minutes): Write what you would do if you were Maya in this exact situation. ¶ Part 4, Rehearse (15 minutes): Play the scene twice: once on the lines only, once playing objectives and subtext. Compare.

hook: Actors look at a scene and ask "What does my character want?" A director looks at the same scene and asks "What is this whole scene about, and how will the audience feel it?" Directing means seeing the big picture.
sc: I can find the meaning and turning point of a scene | I can make blocking and pacing choices that support meaning | I can give clear, respectful notes to actors
v: director :: The person responsible for the overall vision and staging of a production.
v: vision :: The director's overall interpretation of the play.
v: turning point :: The moment in a scene where things change.
v: composition :: How actors are arranged on stage to create a picture.
v: pace :: The speed and rhythm of a scene.
v: note :: Feedback a director gives an actor after a rehearsal.
ts: Seeing Like a Director || Script analysis || How does a director shape a scene's meaning for the audience?
tx: Use a short two-person scene (for example the kitchen scene from the previous lesson or a teacher-chosen scene). ¶ Step 1, The spine (10 minutes): In one sentence, what is the scene about underneath? ("A daughter protects her father from news that could change the family.") ¶ Step 2, Turning point (5 minutes): Mark the moment where the relationship or situation shifts. ¶ Step 3, Stage pictures (15 minutes): Plan three tableaux: opening, turning point and final image. Use levels (sitting, standing), distance (close or far) and focus (where the audience looks) to show the relationship. ¶ Step 4, Pace map (5 minutes): Mark where the scene should speed up, slow down or hold a silence. ¶ Step 5, Direct (20 minutes): Rehearse with two actors. Give notes using the sandwich method only if helpful, but always be specific and actionable: "On 'More hours,' take a beat and look at the letter's hiding place before you speak." ¶ Note-giving rules: speak to the character's action, not the actor's personality; one or two notes at a time; ask questions ("What does Maya want here?").

hook: Twenty-five centuries ago, up to 15,000 Athenians sat on a hillside to watch plays about gods, kings and terrible choices. They invented the words we still use: theatre, drama, chorus, tragedy and comedy.
sc: I can describe the features of Greek theatre | I can explain the role of the chorus and masks | I can connect Greek conventions to theatre today
v: amphitheatre :: A large open-air theatre with curved seating built into a hillside.
v: chorus :: A group of performers who speak, sing or move together and comment on the action.
v: tragedy :: A drama in which the main character suffers a downfall, often through a flaw.
v: comedy :: A drama that ends happily and often mocks people or society.
v: hubris :: Excessive pride that leads to a tragic hero's downfall.
v: catharsis :: The release of emotion the audience feels at the end of a tragedy.
v: convention :: An accepted practice in theatre that the audience understands.
ts: Gods, Masks and the Chorus || Case study and scene work || What did Greek theatre look like, and how does it still shape theatre?
tx: Context: Greek theatre grew from festivals for the god Dionysus in Athens in the 5th century BCE. Playwrights competed for prizes. Famous playwrights: Aeschylus, Sophocles ("Oedipus Rex," "Antigone"), Euripides (tragedy) and Aristophanes (comedy). ¶ The theatre: Open-air amphitheatre with excellent acoustics; the orchestra (circular dancing space) for the chorus; the skene (building behind) for entrances and as a backdrop. ¶ Conventions: Only men performed; actors wore large masks showing character and emotion and helping the voice carry; violence happened offstage and was reported; the chorus (12 to 15 people) commented, questioned and spoke for the community. ¶ Activity 1 (10 minutes): Read the summary of "Antigone": she buries her brother against King Creon's law. Identify the tragic hero, the flaw and the catharsis. ¶ Activity 2, Chorus work (25 minutes): In groups of six, create a choral ode responding to Antigone's arrest. Speak in unison, split lines, use echo and canon, and move as one. Wear neutral half-masks or make simple paper masks.
