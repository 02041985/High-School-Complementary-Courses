hook: Picture two plays. In one, a family argues around a real kitchen table and you feel like you're spying on them. In the other, an actor turns to you and says, "Don't get comfortable, this is a play, and you should be asking why this is happening." Both are powerful. They just want different things from you.
sc: I can describe the features of realism and non-realism | I can compare Stanislavski and Brecht's goals | I can perform a short text in two contrasting styles
v: realism :: A style that shows life as it is, aiming for the audience to believe in the characters.
v: non-realism :: Any style that breaks from showing everyday life, using symbols, exaggeration or direct address.
v: fourth wall :: The imaginary wall between the stage and audience.
v: Brechtian theatre :: Epic theatre developed by Bertolt Brecht to make audiences think critically, not just feel.
v: alienation effect :: Brecht's techniques that remind the audience they are watching a play.
v: absurdism :: A style showing life as meaningless or illogical, as in Beckett's "Waiting for Godot."
ts: One Text, Two Worlds || Scene work || How do realistic and non-realistic styles change how an audience experiences a story?
tx: Realism (late 1800s on): Henrik Ibsen ("A Doll's House"), Anton Chekhov. Real-looking sets, everyday speech, psychological truth, a closed fourth wall. Stanislavski's system was built for this style. ¶ Brechtian (epic) theatre (1920s on): Bertolt Brecht wanted audiences to think about social problems, not get lost in emotion. Techniques: breaking the fourth wall, placards and projected titles, narration, actors playing several roles, showing the stage machinery, songs that interrupt the action. ¶ Absurdism (1950s on): Samuel Beckett, Eugène Ionesco. Repetitive dialogue, circular plots, strange situations, dark humour. ¶ Activity (40 minutes): In pairs, take this short original text about a worker being laid off: ¶ BOSS: We're making some changes. ¶ WORKER: How many changes? ¶ BOSS: One. You. ¶ WORKER: Twelve years. ¶ BOSS: And we're grateful for every one. ¶ Version 1, Realism: Play it truthfully with objectives and subtext. Version 2, Brechtian: Add a placard ("Scene 4: Efficiency"), narrate a stage direction aloud, step out to ask the audience a question. Perform both and discuss the difference.

hook: Every play you've ever seen started as a blank page and one question: "What if...?" What if a girl buried her brother against the king's law? What if two men waited for someone who never came? Playwrights start small, with a character who wants something badly.
sc: I can generate a play idea from a character, a want and an obstacle | I can outline a one-act with a clear dramatic structure | I can write an opening scene with conflict
v: playwright :: A person who writes plays.
v: premise :: The basic idea of a play in one or two sentences.
v: conflict :: The struggle between opposing forces that drives the story.
v: inciting incident :: The event that starts the main conflict.
v: climax :: The moment of highest tension, where the conflict is decided.
v: one-act :: A short play performed without an intermission, usually 10 to 40 minutes.
ts: What If? || Writing workshop || How do playwrights turn a small idea into a dramatic structure?
tx: Part 1, Idea generators (10 minutes): Try three: (a) a headline from local news; (b) a photo of a stranger: who are they, what do they want today?; (c) a "What if...?" question ("What if a teenager found out their grandfather was famous?"). ¶ Part 2, Premise (10 minutes): Choose one. Write: "[Character] wants [goal] but [obstacle], so [what they do]." ¶ Part 3, Structure (15 minutes): Map your one-act using Freytag's pyramid: exposition (world and characters), inciting incident, rising action (three escalating complications), climax, falling action, resolution. Keep it to two or three characters and one location; one-acts work best tight. ¶ Part 4, Opening page (15 minutes): Write the first page. Rules: start late (in the middle of something happening), show conflict within the first page, let dialogue reveal character, keep stage directions brief. ¶ Script format: CHARACTER NAMES in capitals, dialogue below, (stage directions in parentheses and italics).

hook: The set of a production of "Romeo and Juliet" could be an Italian piazza, a modern city with graffiti walls, or a bare stage with two ladders. Same play, completely different meaning. Designers don't decorate a play; they interpret it.
sc: I can develop a design concept from a script | I can communicate design choices through a mood board and rendering | I can explain how design supports the director's vision
v: design concept :: The central idea that guides all design choices for a production.
v: mood board :: A collage of images, colours and textures that captures a design's feeling.
v: rendering :: A drawing or digital image showing what a design will look like.
v: colour palette :: The set of colours chosen for a design.
v: production values :: The overall quality of a production's design and technical elements.
v: theatre criticism :: Writing that describes, analyzes and evaluates a performance.
ts: Designing Meaning || Design task || How do designers turn a script into a visual concept?
tx: Script: choose a play the class knows (for example "Antigone," "A Doll's House" or a student one-act). ¶ Step 1, Find the core (10 minutes): Write three words that capture the play's world and feeling (for example "suffocating, polished, trapped" for "A Doll's House"). ¶ Step 2, Concept statement (10 minutes): One sentence: "This production is set in ___ to show ___." Example: "This production of 'Antigone' is set in a modern surveillance state of grey concrete and cameras to show the power of one voice against control." ¶ Step 3, Mood board (20 minutes): Collect 10 to 15 images, colours, textures and fabric samples that match the concept. ¶ Step 4, Rendering (20 minutes): Choose one area: set, costume or lighting. Make a rendering (sketch or digital) with labels for colours, materials and key choices. ¶ Step 5, Pitch (5 minutes per group): Present your concept and explain how each choice connects to the play's themes. ¶ Critic's corner: After watching a live or recorded production, write a short review: describe, analyze how design supported meaning, evaluate with evidence.
