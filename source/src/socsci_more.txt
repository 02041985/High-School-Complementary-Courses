tx: The neuron: Messages arrive at the dendrites, travel through the cell body, then down the axon as an electrical impulse (action potential). The myelin sheath insulates the axon and speeds the signal. At the axon terminals, neurotransmitters are released into the synapse and bind to receptors on the next neuron. ¶ Key neurotransmitters: dopamine (reward and movement), serotonin (mood, sleep), acetylcholine (muscle movement, memory), endorphins (pain relief). ¶ Brain structures: frontal lobe (planning, decisions, personality; still developing into the mid-20s), parietal lobe (touch, spatial awareness), temporal lobe (hearing, language comprehension, memory), occipital lobe (vision), cerebellum (balance and coordination), brainstem (breathing, heart rate), limbic system including the amygdala (fear, emotion) and hippocampus (forming memories). ¶ Case: Phineas Gage (1848), a railway worker, survived an iron rod passing through his frontal lobe. hook: Every time your phone buzzes, you probably reach for it before thinking. You weren't born doing that. You learned it, through the same process a Russian scientist discovered while studying dogs that drooled at the sound of footsteps.
sc: I can explain classical conditioning using its key terms | I can identify the parts of conditioning in real examples | I can explain extinction and generalization
v: classical conditioning :: Learning in which a neutral stimulus becomes linked with a meaningful one and triggers a response.
v: unconditioned stimulus (UCS) :: Something that naturally triggers a response.
v: unconditioned response (UCR) :: The natural response to the UCS.
v: conditioned stimulus (CS) :: A once-neutral stimulus that now triggers a response after learning.
v: conditioned response (CR) :: The learned response to the CS.
v: extinction :: When a conditioned response fades because the CS no longer predicts the UCS.
v: generalization :: Responding to stimuli similar to the CS.
ts: Pavlov's Discovery || Case study || How do we learn to connect events?
tx: Ivan Pavlov (1890s–1900s) was studying digestion in dogs when he noticed they drooled before food arrived, at the sound of the assistant's footsteps. He tested it: ¶ • Before learning: food (UCS) → drooling (UCR); bell (neutral) → no drooling. ¶ • During learning: bell + food, many times. ¶ • After learning: bell (CS) → drooling (CR). ¶ Extinction: If the bell rings again and again with no food, drooling fades. Spontaneous recovery: After a rest, the bell may trigger drooling again briefly. Generalization: A similar sound triggers drooling. Discrimination: The dog learns to respond only to the exact bell. ¶ Watson and "Little Albert" (1920): John B. Watson conditioned a baby to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise; the fear generalized to other furry things. The study is now considered deeply unethical: no consent from informed parents in the modern sense, distress to a child and no deconditioning. ¶ Everyday examples: phone notification sounds, the smell of a hospital, a song that reminds you of someone, ads pairing products with happy music. ¶ Task: Label UCS, UCR, CS and CR in six scenarios.

v: personality :: A person's consistent patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving.
v: trait :: A stable characteristic, such as being organized or outgoing.
v: Big Five :: The five broad traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism (OCEAN).
v: psychodynamic :: Freud's approach focusing on unconscious drives and early experiences.
v: humanistic :: An approach focusing on free will and personal growth, from Maslow and Rogers.
v: reliability :: Whether a test gives consistent results.
v: validity :: Whether a test measures what it claims to measure.
ts: Who Are You? || Comparison task || How do different theories explain personality?
tx: Four approaches: ¶ • Psychodynamic (Sigmund Freud): personality shaped by unconscious conflict between the id (desires), ego (reality) and superego (conscience), and by early childhood. Influential but hard to test scientifically. ¶ • Humanistic (Abraham Maslow, Carl Rogers): people are basically good and strive for self-actualization; personality grows with acceptance and a healthy self-concept. ¶ • Trait (Big Five, OCEAN): Openness (curious vs traditional), Conscientiousness (organized vs careless), Extraversion (outgoing vs reserved), Agreeableness (cooperative vs competitive), Neuroticism (anxious vs calm). Supported by much research across cultures; traits are partly heritable and fairly stable but can shift with age. ¶ • Social-cognitive (Albert Bandura): personality comes from interaction of thoughts, behaviour and environment; self-efficacy (belief in your ability) matters. ¶ Test check: Many online "personality quizzes" are not reliable or valid. Some, like popular four-letter type tests, have weaker scientific support than the Big Five. ¶ Task: Take a short Big Five inventory (teacher-provided). Then explain one behaviour of a fictional character using all four approaches.
