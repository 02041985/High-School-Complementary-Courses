ts: How Children Think || Case study and demonstration || How does thinking change from infancy to adolescence?
tx: Jean Piaget (Swiss, 1896–1980) observed his own and other children and proposed four stages: ¶ 1. Sensorimotor (birth–2 years): learning through senses and movement; object permanence develops around 8 months. ¶ 2. Preoperational (2–7): language and pretend play grow; egocentrism (the "three mountains" task: children describe what they see, not what a doll across the table sees); lacks conservation. ¶ 3. Concrete operational (7–11): logical thinking about concrete things; conservation; can sort and classify. ¶ 4. Formal operational (12+): abstract and hypothetical reasoning ("What if...?"), scientific thinking. ¶ Schemas grow through assimilation (a toddler calls a cow a "doggy") and accommodation (learning cows are different). ¶ Demonstration: The conservation of liquid task. Pour equal water into two identical glasses; then pour one into a tall, thin glass. A preoperational child says the tall glass has more. ¶ Evaluation: Strengths: shaped education and showed children think differently, not just less. Limits: children can show abilities earlier than Piaget thought; stages may be less fixed; culture affects development (Vygotsky stressed social learning).

ts: The Power of the Group || Case study || How do other people influence what we do?
tx: Study 1, Asch's conformity experiment (1951): Participants judged which of three lines matched a target line. Confederates (actors) gave obviously wrong answers. About 75% of participants conformed at least once; overall about one third of answers conformed. Conformity dropped sharply when even one other person gave the right answer. ¶ Study 2, Milgram's obedience experiment (1963): Participants were told to give increasing "electric shocks" to a learner (an actor; no real shocks) for wrong answers. About 65% continued to the maximum 450 volts when the experimenter said "Please continue." Obedience dropped when the authority was absent or a peer refused. Ethics: participants experienced serious stress and were deceived; today's ethics rules would not allow it in this form. ¶ Study 3, Bystander effect (Darley and Latané, 1968): Participants who believed others could hear a person having a seizure were less likely and slower to help. Diffusion of responsibility explains it. ¶ Factors that increase conformity and obedience: group size (up to about 4–5), unanimity, authority's status and closeness, uncertainty, culture. ¶ Task: Apply these ideas to three scenarios: a group chat pressuring someone, a supervisor ordering an unsafe shortcut, and a crowd watching someone fall.
