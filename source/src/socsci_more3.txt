hook: In some cultures, it's polite to finish everything on your plate; in others, it suggests the host didn't serve enough. Nobody is born knowing these rules. We learn them, and sociologists call that process socialization.
sc: I can define culture, norms and values | I can distinguish folkways, mores and laws | I can explain how agents of socialization teach culture
v: culture :: The shared beliefs, values, behaviours and material objects of a group.
v: norm :: A shared rule or expectation for behaviour.
v: value :: A shared idea about what is good, right or important.
v: folkway :: An informal norm about everyday behaviour (for example, facing forward in an elevator).
v: more :: A serious norm based on moral views (pronounced "mor-ay").
v: socialization :: The lifelong process of learning a culture.
v: agent of socialization :: A person or group that teaches us culture, such as family, peers, school or media.

hook: You belong to dozens of groups: your family, your friend group, your class, your team, maybe a group chat with 200 people you've never met. Some shape who you are; others you'd barely notice leaving. Sociologists explain why.
sc: I can distinguish primary and secondary groups | I can describe roles, status and in-groups and out-groups | I can explain how groups influence individuals
v: social group :: Two or more people who interact and share a sense of belonging.
v: primary group :: A small, close, long-lasting group, such as family or close friends.
v: secondary group :: A larger, goal-oriented, less personal group, such as a class or workplace.
v: status :: A social position a person holds.
v: role :: The behaviour expected of someone with a status.
v: in-group :: A group you belong to and feel loyal to.
v: out-group :: A group you don't belong to and may feel competitive toward.
ts: Map My Groups || Investigation || How do groups shape who we are?
tx: Part 1, Group map (15 minutes): Draw yourself in the centre. Draw circles for every group you belong to; place the closest ones nearest you. Label each as primary or secondary. ¶ Charles Horton Cooley (1909) called primary groups the "nursery of human nature": we get identity and emotional support from them. Secondary groups exist for a purpose: earning money, learning, playing a sport. A secondary group can become primary (teammates who become close friends). ¶ Part 2, Status and roles (10 minutes): List your statuses (student, sibling, team captain, employee). Ascribed status: given at birth (age, family). Achieved status: earned (captain, honour student). For two statuses, list expected roles. Role conflict: when two roles clash (a big game the same night as a family event). ¶ Part 3, In-groups and out-groups (10 minutes): Discuss school rivalries. How does belonging to an in-group affect how you see out-groups? Robbers Cave study (Sherif, 1954): two groups of boys at camp quickly became hostile through competition and became friendlier after cooperating on shared goals. ¶ Part 4, Group size (10 minutes): Dyads (two people) are intimate but fragile; triads (three) are more stable but can form two-against-one coalitions.

hook: In some times and places, women wearing pants was considered deviant. So was a man with long hair, or a teenager with a tattoo. Deviance isn't about the act itself. It's about how a society reacts to it, and that changes.
sc: I can define deviance as relative to time, place and group | I can describe formal and informal social control | I can explain theories of deviance such as labelling and strain theory
v: deviance :: Behaviour that breaks a group's norms.
v: social control :: The ways a society encourages conformity to norms.
v: formal sanction :: An official reward or punishment, such as a fine or a diploma.
v: informal sanction :: An unofficial reaction, such as a compliment or an eye-roll.
v: labelling theory :: The idea that being labelled deviant can lead people to see themselves that way and act on it.
v: strain theory :: Robert Merton's idea that deviance happens when people can't reach accepted goals by accepted means.
ts: Who Decides What's Deviant? || Investigation || How do societies define and control deviance?
tx: Part 1, Relative deviance (10 minutes): Rate these from 1 (not deviant) to 5 (very deviant): dyeing your hair blue, talking during a movie, cheating on a test, going barefoot in a mall, eating with your hands, refusing to stand for the national anthem. Compare ratings. Then ask: would the ratings change in another country, another decade or another group? ¶ Part 2, Social control (10 minutes): Sort sanctions into formal and informal, positive and negative: a speeding ticket, a high-five, being grounded, a scholarship, a dirty look, a medal, being fired. ¶ Part 3, Theories (15 minutes): ¶ • Functionalism (Durkheim): deviance clarifies norms and can lead to change. ¶ • Strain theory (Merton): society values success (goal) but not everyone has access to education or jobs (means). Responses: conformity, innovation (new illegal means), ritualism, retreatism, rebellion. ¶ • Labelling theory (Becker): once labelled "troublemaker," a person may be treated differently and accept the label. ¶ Part 4, Case (10 minutes): A student is labelled a "bad kid" in Grade 6 after a fight. Use labelling theory to explain how this label could shape later years. What could interrupt it?
